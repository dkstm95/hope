# Exploration and feedback

## Find the useful uncertainty

Begin with what is already known. Separate a product decision (what people can
do or see), a visual direction (how the experience communicates), and a local
adjustment. Only reopen a settled choice when new evidence changes its basis.

| Impression | Investigate before prescribing |
|---|---|
| Cramped | Group separation, content measure, target spacing, density by region, repeated navigation |
| Bland | Hierarchy, focal content, proportions, rhythm, image quality, strength of the chosen identity |
| Loud | Competing emphasis, repetitive labels, saturation, scale, simultaneous motion |
| Cheap | Inconsistent materials, weak assets, accidental spacing, conflicting type and icon weights |
| Confusing | Vocabulary, action cues, reading order, hidden state, control-to-result mapping |
| Slow or heavy | Actual latency, late feedback, long transitions, layout shifts, rendering cost |

These are hypotheses. Use the screen and task to distinguish them. Do not turn
the table into automatic CSS substitutions. If feedback could mean different
material changes, demonstrate a narrow contrast or ask a concrete question.

## Read references as evidence

Use supplied references and existing project screens first. Seek outside
references when they resolve a missing direction; don't require a gallery trip
for a clear local request. Find comparable behavior as well as appearance.
Marketing galleries can inform expression but may omit real product states.

For a page, inspect the relevant region and, when material, another width, theme,
or interaction. For an effect, investigate its contributing layers: pseudo-elements,
masks, gradients, images, SVG, transforms, stacking, or canvas. Read computed
styles and source where available. A token declaration alone does not establish
which style rendered; inaccessible stylesheets and runtime effects leave gaps.

For an image, describe observable composition, relative spacing, color, and type
character. Do not claim to identify exact fonts, CSS, interaction, or responsive
behavior from pixels. Mark measurements, derived relationships, and inferred
intent separately. Explain what transfers to the current task and what does not.

If a new direction is too close to a generic category template, explore the
audience's documents, tools, physical objects, editorial traditions, or visual
notation. Borrow a useful organizing principle, not an arbitrary costume.
Do not require novelty or force a fixed candidate count or random choice.

## Compare and refine

Name what the comparison tests before building. A local test should isolate the
main cause: density, hierarchy, layout, tone, or motion. Whole directions can
change several connected properties; state that difference rather than claiming
a controlled experiment. Give alternatives realistic content, equal care, and
comparable viewing conditions. Do not make the preferred option win by omitting
content or states from its competitors.

Record feedback against the shown version, direction, region, and state. A
region name or annotation is sufficient; the person need not identify a DOM
selector. Keep their wording alongside your interpretation when ambiguity
matters. Do not apply feedback from an old view as if it describes the new one.

When combining options, preserve the accepted qualities and reconcile the whole:
navigation, density, type, colors, components, and motion must still agree.
Explain a real conflict between requested parts instead of silently dropping one.
Make the next revision answer the feedback rather than exposing every pixel
choice for approval. If successive attempts do not help, revisit the diagnosis,
reference, or task understanding before generating more.

Use short explanations to help the person judge their own work. Do not turn a
design session into a terminology lesson, long questionnaire, or repeated
confirmation of unchanged choices. A supplied precise design or explicit
delegation can settle a branch immediately.
