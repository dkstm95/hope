# Design judgment

## Start with the job

Identify what the screen helps someone do. Its purpose may change by region:
operating a tool, reading, comparing alternatives, understanding a product, or
exploring an experience. Use actual tasks, content, frequency, input methods,
and surroundings to choose the composition. An admin screen and its marketing
site do not need the same density, typography, or motion.

For repeated work, preserve context and familiar controls. For reading, prioritize
measure, hierarchy, navigation, and source clarity. For comparison, align the
attributes people compare. For persuasion, show the real product and evidence
in an intelligible sequence. For expressive work, choose a coherent visual idea
and let the subject lead. Distinctiveness has to serve this purpose.

Follow the path from arrival through the principal action to completion or
recovery. Make current status, available actions, and their consequences visible.
Put controls near their effects. Prefer recognition over making someone remember
information across screens. Reveal secondary complexity where it becomes useful,
with a visible way to find it. Familiarity, density, and standard components can
be strengths; novelty and empty space are not universal improvements.

## Compose before polishing

Decide what deserves attention first, next, and on demand. Give these roles
different visual weight through position, grouping, space, type, contrast, and
content. Increasing every title or metric flattens hierarchy. Repeated items may
share a structure, but unrelated jobs need not share identical cards.

Inspect the whole surface at its intended size. Temporarily attend to masses,
alignment, and contrast rather than reading each sentence. Can someone locate
the main content and likely action? Then read closely to verify that the visual
order agrees with the information's meaning. This is a diagnostic aid, not a
score or a requirement that every screen have one oversized focal element.

Treat "AI-looking" as a set of possible failures to investigate:

- repeated category labels, oversized headings, and explanatory prose compete
  with the actual task;
- every section uses the same icon/title/card skeleton regardless of content;
- accent colors, gradients, borders, badges, and motion mark everything;
- generic imagery or empty containers substitute for the subject;
- fonts, controls, icon families, radii, or states contradict each other;
- the opening screen is polished while the remaining path is unfinished.

Fix the cause. Use grouping instead of redundant containers, lower the emphasis
of metadata, remove prose that repeats an obvious action, and replace ornamental
placeholders with meaningful content. Do not remove field names, units, status,
current location, or accessible names merely because they are labels.

A quiet result can still be wrong: all-gray text with no hierarchy, weak contrast,
tiny controls, or a sea of equal cards. A bold result can be right: substantial
type, vivid color, unusual composition, or motion grounded in the brief. Preserve
deliberate choices and the parts the person likes. Do not impose a universal
typeface, accent ratio, card ban, or maximum heading size.

## Make trade-offs legible

Describe what changes for the user: "More rows stay visible, but scanning each
row takes more care" or "The detail stays beside the list, but needs more width."
Recommend with task evidence, not a declaration that one aesthetic is superior.
State when a judgment is inferred from an image or has not been tried.

Use a small range of credible alternatives to test a real uncertainty. A new
visual identity may need broader reference exploration; a spacing correction
does not. Quality comes from composition, content, and execution together, not
the number of options or rules loaded.
